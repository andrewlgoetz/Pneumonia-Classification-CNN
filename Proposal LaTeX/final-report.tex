\section{Introduction}

% Here, write a brief introduction to the problem you are solving. This can be adapted from your problem description and motivation from the original proposal. This should be around 0.25-0.5 pages.
Pneumonia is a severe respiratory infection causing significant morbidity and mortality worldwide, especially in young children. Accurate and timely diagnosis is crucial for effective treatment and 
improving outcomes. Traditionally, diagnosis relies on expert radiologists interpreting chest X-rays, but deep learning–based automated detection systems offer potential for faster and more scalable assessment that can support clinicians in high-volume or low-resource settings.

In this project, we focus on pediatric chest X-ray images from a publicly available dataset, where each image is labeled as either Normal or Bacterial Pneumonia. Framing the problem as a binary classification task reflects a clinically meaningful distinction: separating healthy children from those with clear evidence of bacterial infection that may require urgent treatment. At the same time, working with pediatric data introduces challenges such as smaller dataset size, higher variability in anatomy and acquisition conditions, and the need for robust models that generalize beyond the training distribution.

Our team developed a convolutional neural network (CNN)–based model that distinguishes between normal and bacterial pneumonia pediatric chest X-ray images using transfer learning with pretrained weights. By starting from an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 backbone and carefully fine-tuning it on this medical domain, we aim to leverage powerful generic visual features while adapting them to lung-specific patterns such as consolidations and infiltrates. We complement this with strong data augmentation, class imbalance handling, and systematic evaluation against simple baselines to build a practical and interpretable pneumonia detection system. Lastly, we also included a new threshold optimization to improve recall performance of the model.


\section{Related Work}

% Here, talk about the related work you encountered for your approach. Cite at least 5 references. Refer to item 2. No one has done exactly your task? Write about the most similar thing you can find. This should be around 0.25-0.5 pages.

One of the most influential works in this area is CheXNet by Rajpurkar et al., who trained a 121-layer DenseNet on a much larger dataset "NIH ChestX-ray14 dataset" (over 100,000 adult radiographs with 14 labels) and showed that their model could detect pneumonia at or above average radiologist performance on a held-out test set (for certain pathologies). They later extended this approach to CheXNeXt, a multi-label model that jointly predicts multiple thoracic pathologies from a single chest X-ray (Rajpurkar, 2018). These works popularized the use of deep, CNNs on large CXR datasets. We drew inspiration from this paradigm our own model.


Previous work done on a similar task (same dataset) by (Kermany, 2018) has shown the effectiveness of both Convolutional Neural Networks and transfer learning in the domain medical image classification managing to achieve accuracies of >90\% on multi-label classification problems using Optical Coherence Tomography Images with Choroidal Neovascularization(CNV)/Drusen/Diabetic Macular Edema(DME); and Chest X-Rays with either Viral/Bacterial Pneumonia.  According to (Chouhan, 2020) transfer learning is a great tool for domains when only limited data is available, a major issue faced in using these models is that they are quite large compared to the dataset and when fine tuning deeper layers they can easily overfit to the training set. Our work can be seen as a more focused reproduction/extension of this line of work, with additional emphasis on threshold tuning and detailed error analysis


A contrasting line of work to the transfer-learning paradigm uses classical machine learning on hand-crafted features rather than deep CNNs. (Nagashree and Mahanand, 2023) present a system for pneumonia chest X-ray classification using a Support Vector Machine (SVM), targeting the same type of task as ours: distinguishing pneumonia from normal cases in chest radiographs. Instead of learning features from scratch, they first extract Haar, shape, and texture descriptors from each X-ray and then train an SVM on these fixed feature vectors. Their study compares SVM performance against several other classifiers (decision trees, naive Bayes, k-nearest neighbours) and reports that SVM achieves the best overall accuracy for pneumonia detection. This pipeline is an example of a non–transfer-learning approach: all discriminative information comes from manually engineered features, and no pretrained deep network or ImageNet-style initialization is used.

Another strand of medical image classification work explicitly questions the value of “standard” transfer learning and argues for more task-specific architectures. For example, (Hosny, 2025) propose an attention-enhanced DenseNet framework for multiclass gastrointestinal disease classification that they describe as going “beyond transfer learning”: instead of simply fine-tuning a pretrained backbone, they augment it with additional Inception, residual, and attention blocks tailored to GI imagery. Their results suggest that naïvely reusing large ImageNet-pretrained models can be overly generic and computationally heavy, and that adapting architectures to the structure of medical data can yield better clinical utility. This perspective is consistent with our own finding that a relatively shallow ResNet-18 and even a simple CNN can outperform a deeper ResNet-50 on pediatric chest X-rays, and it points toward future extensions of our work that incorporate lightweight attention or task-specific modules rather than relying solely on off-the-shelf transfer learning.

\section{Dataset}

% You should write about your dataset here, following the guidelines regarding item 1. This section may be 0.5-1 pages. Depending on your specific dataset, you may want to include subsections for the preprocessing, annotation, etc.

We use the publicly available pediatric chest X-ray dataset sourced from Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center. This dataset contains over 5,800 frontal chest X-ray images of pediatric patients aged between 1 and 5 years. Images are categorized as Normal, Viral Pneumonia, or Bacterial Pneumonia. For our project, we focus on distinguishing Normal images from those showing Bacterial Pneumonia, making it a binary classification task aligned with clinical priorities.

The dataset we received has already undergone thorough quality control and was expertly annotated by radiologists before release. Poor-quality scans or unreadable images were excluded, and all images bear reliable labels confirmed by multiple reviewers. This ensures that the dataset is well-suited for training without requiring additional manual cleanup from our side.

We use the original train/test split provided with the dataset and further create a stratified 90/10 split of the training set to obtain a validation set, preserving label proportions across train/validation/test.

Each image is resized to a fixed \(224 \times 224\) pixel resolution. This size was chosen to balance model input requirements, computational efficiency, and sufficient preservation of pulmonary detail crucial for diagnosis. Pixel intensities are normalized using ImageNet channel mean and standard deviation statistics, a standard practice that promotes better convergence when fine-tuning pretrained convolutional neural networks.

To increase training data diversity and reduce overfitting, we apply an extended augmentation pipeline. During training, each image undergoes a random resized crop (scale 0.8–1.0), random horizontal flip (p = 0.5), small random rotations (±10°), colour jitter (brightness/contrast/saturation/hue up to 0.2), and additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma$ approx. 0.01). These augmentations broaden the range of plausible acquisition conditions and patient positioning, encouraging the model to learn robust, invariant features rather than memorizing specific pixel patterns. Compared to the progress report, this richer augmentation set was introduced to support high-recall.

This method allowed us to attribute performance differences to the quality of learned features rather than changes in preprocessing.

\section{Implementation}

% Describe your model and implementation here. Refer to item 4. This may take around a page.


The model uses a ResNet18 convolutional neural network pretrained on ImageNet, adapted for binary classification of pediatric chest X-rays into Normal and Bacterial Pneumonia categories. We preprocess images by resizing to 224$\times$224 pixels (increased from the previous size of 160$\times$160 to match ResNet's standard input), converting grayscale to three-channel RGB by repeating channels, and normalizing with ImageNet mean (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and standard deviation (0.229, 0.224, 0.225) values. This normalization subtracts the channel-wise mean and divides by the standard deviation from the ImageNet training distribution, ensuring the pretrained ResNet18 receives inputs with the same statistical properties it expects, which stabilizes training and preserves the value of pretrained weights even on grayscale medical images.


Initially, we freeze all pretrained layers except the final fully connected layer, which is replaced to suit our binary pneumonia classification task . In this stage (Phase A), only the classification head is trained using Adam optimizer (initial lr=1e-3, weight decay=1e-4), enabling the network to learn the mapping from extracted features to target labels while keeping the general feature extractor unchanged and preserving low-level ImageNet features like edges and textures.

To address class imbalance in the dataset (3:1 pneumonia:normal), we use a weighted cross-entropy loss including label smoothing (0.05), formulated as:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = - \sum_{i=1}^N w_{y_i} \log p(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i)
\label{eq:weighted_cross_entropy}
\end{equation}

Here, \(N\) is the number of samples, \(y_i\) is the true label of sample \(i\), \(w_{y_i}\) is the corresponding class weight compensating for imbalance, and \(p(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) is the predicted probability for the correct class given input \(\mathbf{x}_i\).

Phase A applies extensive data augmentations, including random resized crops (scale 0.8--1.0), 
horizontal flips ($p=0.5$), rotations ($\pm 10^\circ$), color jitter 
(brightness/contrast/saturation/hue up to 0.2), and Gaussian noise ($\sigma=0.01$), 
doubling augmentation variety from our previous milestone to combat overfitting 
on approximately $5{,}000$ pediatric samples. 

Training uses a \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} scheduler that halves the learning rate 
after 5 epochs of no validation loss improvement (patience=5, factor=0.5, 
min\_lr $= 10^{-6}$), with early stopping based on the validation F1-score 
(patience=10) to halt training when improvements plateau, ensuring the preservation 
of the best model weights. 

Mixed-precision training provides 1.5--2$\times$ speedup and reduces memory usage. 
Phase A achieved best validation F1 = 0.89.



Once the classification head achieves satisfactory performance, we unfreeze the last convolutional block (Phase B: layers 3-5 of ResNet-18's four blocks, ~20\% of parameters) to fine-tune deeper layers at reduced lr=1e-4 for up to 50 epochs. This process allows the network to adapt more task-specific features relevant to pediatric chest X-rays and pneumonia detection, while preserving useful pretrained representations. We apply a reduced learning rate during fine-tuning to stabilize training. Phase B pushed validation F1 to 0.95.

Training is performed using the Adam optimizer with weight decay to regularize model parameters 
and reduce overfitting. Our modular \texttt{run\_epoch()} function handles both phases uniformly: 
training/evaluation modes, automatic gradient accumulation/scaling, and batch-wise metric tracking 
(accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and confusion matrices). Best models are automatically 
saved when the validation F1 improves.


For baseline comparison, a simple majority-class classifier is implemented, which always predicts the most frequent class in the training set. This baseline sets a minimal performance bar that our trained model is expected to exceed.
Data augmentation and preprocessing improved robustness, alongside stratified sampling for balanced batches, dynamic class-weight updates, and automated generation of loss/F1 plots and labeled confusion matrices. Hyperparameter tuning explored learning rates from 1e-3 to 1e-5, batch sizes of 16–64, and dropout rates of 0–0.5, guided by validation F1. Challenges encountered include tuning when to freeze or unfreeze layers, selecting appropriate learning rates, and optimizing the threshold rate, which were addressed through iterative experimentation guided by validation metrics.

The modular design of the implementation facilitates easy extension with alternative models, loss functions, or augmentation methods in future work. Overall, this enhanced transfer learning approach provides a strong framework for pediatric pneumonia
classification, boosting validation F1 from 0.89 (Phase A) to 0.95 (Phase B), directly addressing TA feedback on baselines and systematic evaluation.

\subsection{Threshold Tuning}

From there, we systematically swept over thresholds and plotted the precision–recall (PR) curve for the positive (pneumonia) class, treating each point on the curve as a different error profile. This allowed us to see, for each candidate threshold, how many pneumonia cases we would recover (recall) and how many false alarms we would generate (precision).

Initially, with our earlier model and augmentation pipeline, achieving approx. 98\% recall (we acheived between 96 and 98 \% depending on randomness) required pushing the threshold down to about 0.06, which drove precision down to roughly 0.78. The corresponding confusion matrices and PR-curve position made it clear that this operating point produced an unacceptable number of false positives: the model would flag many normal images as pneumonia to catch nearly all pneumonia cases. Guided by these diagnostics, we revisited the implementation (as described in the previous section), strengthening data augmentation and adding a second fine-tuning phase to improve the underlying representation rather than simply “turning the threshold knob.”

Another way our team investigated systemically analyzed our errors was through the process of developing various models of similar architectures to help us understand which parts of the CNN / Transfer-Learning Architecture is driving performance of Pneumonia Detection. The models we used to compare and evaluate were a: Majority Classifier baseline, a simple CNN baseline, a ResNet-50 Fine Tuned model, and our own ResNet-18 Model. We compare all the same metrics in our main model in Table~\ref{tab:model_performance} , those being: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, as well as plotting a Confusion Matrix. We further contextualize the results and conclusions we drew from our different model investigations with additional research into CNN articles, that helped us understand the variations in the different performance metrics. 


Furthering upon our reflection from the progress report, we implemented a strong yet still relatively simpler CNN from scratch that didn’t implement any form of transfer learning. All convolutional layers used a kernel size of 3. The CNN baseline consisted of a 3-channel input to a Convolutional Layer of 10 computational units, passed to a ReLU activation then a 2x2 Max Pooling layer. Next, another convolutional layer that had 5 computational units followed by a ReLU activation, and then another convolutional layer with 16 computational units and a ReLU activation, followed by another 2x2 Max pooling layer. The classifying head following the max pooling layer comprised of a 21,904 input Fully connected layer with 128 outputs and a ReLU activation, a 64 output Fully connected layer followed by a ReLU, and lastly a Fully connected layer that produced a binary output corresponding to Pneumonia or Normal. We apply the same Data preprocessing and Augmentation as we do in our main model, so that we may attribute differences in performance to architecture and help us better understand the impact transfer learning has while minimizing the effect differences in input would produce. 

The architecture is not too unfamiliar to other models that follow a Convolution Layer, some Detector Layer, then some Pooling Layer as part of their feature extraction, and fully connected layers for the classifying head, this model has been inspired by other deep CNNs made for image classification such as VGG which shares a similar architecture \citep{SimonyanZisserman2014}.  Despite the model parameters being chosen at random it had sufficient capacity to perform effectively scoring an accuracy of 0.888, a precision of 0.874, a recall of 0.956, a F1-Score of 0.914, and a ROC-AUC score of 0.939. We believe these scores are more likely to do with our exhaustive preprocessing and data augmentation which was also paired with a model built for simple edge and feature extraction leading to results surpassing our expectation.  We are not too surprised it managed to perform that well and believe this sets a high bar for our final model’s metrics. Though the performance of this model suggests that transfer learning may or be providing minuscule benefit in this scenario of pneumonia detection as its highly specialized and image-nets train on classifying a wide variety of classes with ResNet-18 originally being able to classify up to 1000 different general labels.

They concluded that transfer learning image classifying models that used pretrained ImageNet weights only need the first two layers in most cases. They also explain how medical imaging datasets are vastly different compared to those like ImageNet where the subject of the image is in clear unobstructed view compared to X-rays where there’s constantly varying background and the area of interest is hidden in the variations of local textures. This article’s observations fully support our own prior results. Our simple CNN baseline managed to achieve a higher performance than our ResNet-50 model. From that investigation, we concluded that the pretrained image models and the features they are extracting are too generic, while only the lower-level layers are performing important feature extraction. Despite this we committed to the implementation of the shallower ResNet-18 model since our model fine-tuning seemed to work in our favor such that it surpassed the metrics of all our baselines.

\subsection{Strengths and Weaknesses}
From our experiments, the model is clearly good at detecting pneumonia and maintaining high sensitivity once we tune the threshold, but it still struggles with a subset of borderline or atypical normal cases.

After error-driven tuning and stronger augmentation, lowering the threshold to 0.20 shifts the error profile: recall rises to approx. 0.96 with only 15 false negatives, while precision remains approx. 0.93 and accuracy approx. 0.93. This tells us the model is very good at picking up pneumonia-like patterns (infiltrates, consolidations, diffuse opacities) once we let it be more “sensitive,” and that many previously missed cases were near the decision boundary rather than fundamentally confusing to the network.

The trade-off for optimizing for recall is that at 0.20 we see more false positives (28 vs 10 at 0.5). Comparing confusion matrices and PR curves across models, a pattern emerges: \\
- \textbf{The simple CNN} has high recall but more false positives and slightly lower overall discrimination (lower ROC–AUC). \\
- \textbf{ResNet-50} achieves strong ROC–AUC but lower recall, suggesting it overfits or attends to less clinically relevant cues. \\
- \textbf{The final ResNet-18 with tuned threshold} gives the best balance: its remaining errors are mostly normals that are “pneumonia-like” in texture or imaging conditions, rather than clear-cut normals.

If we were to continue, we would target these issues on several fronts. For false negatives, we could rebalance or reweight particularly hard pneumonia cases, or use curriculum learning / hard-example mining so the network repeatedly sees borderline positives. For false positives, we could design normal-focused augmentations (e.g., adding lines, devices, exposure changes) to teach the model that these artefacts do not imply disease. Manual feature engineering could also be explored: for example, adding simple lung-field segmentation masks, lung/heart shape descriptors, or handcrafted contrast/texture features as auxiliary inputs might help the model distinguish true parenchymal changes from global noise. More structurally, incorporating attention or localization mechanisms (e.g., supervision on lung regions) or pretraining on large chest-X-ray datasets could reduce reliance on spurious textures. Finally, k-fold cross-validation on the training data would give a more robust view of how stable these error patterns are across different splits, helping us distinguish genuine model weaknesses from artifacts of a single train/validation partition and guiding which error modes to prioritize in future iterations.
